A walkway is the first thing your house says

A walkway is the first surface anyone touches on your property, and in Dallas it is also the hardest working one. It carries foot traffic, absorbs August heat that regularly pushes surface temperatures past 140°F, sheds sudden spring downpours, and sits on some of the most active expansive clay in the country. A path that was poured without thought about any of that will tell on itself within two or three seasons: hairline cracks widening into gaps, one panel lifting above the next, a lip at the front step that catches the toe of a shoe. Concrete done properly does the opposite — it settles into the landscape, holds its line, and quietly raises the whole front elevation of the home.

That is the standard we build to on every walkway across Dallas and the surrounding suburbs, whether it is a straight twelve-foot run from the driveway to a front door in Lakewood or a curved garden path threading between raised beds in Preston Hollow. The work starts long before anyone mixes concrete: reading how water moves across the yard, checking the fall from the house, deciding where control joints have to land so that the inevitable shrinkage happens on a clean line instead of a random one. Built for North Texas clay and heat

Dallas sits on Houston Black and Austin Chalk clays — soils that swell when wet and shrink hard when dry. Over a single year the ground under a walkway can move an inch or more vertically. Concrete does not fight that movement; it is detailed to accommodate it.

Soil movement and jointing

Control joints cut at roughly two to three times the slab thickness in feet — for a 4" walkway, every 8 to 10 feet — give shrinkage cracking a predetermined place to go. Isolation joints separate the walkway from the house slab, porch and driveway so each element can move independently instead of pulling on its neighbour.

Water, heat and surface texture

Every run is graded to shed water away from the foundation, usually a quarter inch of fall per foot. Light broom and sandblasted finishes stay cooler underfoot than dark stamped colours and keep their grip when a thunderstorm rolls through, which matters on any path that leads to a pool gate or a shaded side yard that dries slowly.

Finishes that suit the house, not the catalogue

The finish is where a poured path stops looking utilitarian. Texture, colour and joint pattern are chosen against the brick, stone and roofline already on the property — a limestone-clad traditional in Highland Park asks for something different than a white-rendered modern build in Bishop Arts.

Broom finish

The dependable default. A light drag of the broom perpendicular to travel gives reliable traction, hides minor surface variation and ages evenly. Best value per square foot.

Exposed aggregate

The top paste is washed back to reveal the stone inside the mix. Rich texture, excellent slip resistance, and a natural warmth that sits well beside planting beds and stone facades.

Stamped concrete

Pressed patterns — ashlar slate, running bond brick, cut stone — with integral colour and release. The look of pavers with a continuous, weed-free surface.

Saw-cut panels

Clean geometric panels cut after cure, often with grass or gravel joints between. The choice for contemporary homes where the walkway reads as architecture.

Sandblasted

A fine, uniform matte texture achieved by abrading the surface. Understated, cool underfoot, and forgiving of the strong Texas sun.

Banded borders

A contrasting stamped or coloured band framing a plain field. Defines the edge of the path and visually widens a narrow run.

How the walkway gets built

Six stages, in order, on every job. Nothing here is optional and nothing gets compressed to save a day.

01

Site walk and layout

We stake the route, confirm width, check fall away from the foundation, and mark utilities. Curves are laid out with a hose on the ground so you see the real line before anything is dug.

02

Excavation and sub-base

Topsoil and loose clay are removed to depth, then a crushed stone base is placed and compacted in lifts. On highly active soil we go deeper and add base rather than trusting the clay.

03

Forms and reinforcement

Forms are set to line and grade, and the slab is reinforced with fibre mesh or #3 rebar on chairs depending on span, traffic and soil conditions.

04

Pour and finish

Concrete is placed, screeded, floated and finished to the selected texture. Edges are tooled, and stamping or aggregate exposure happens inside a tight window while the slab is still workable.

05

Joints and curing

Control joints are cut at the planned spacing, then the slab is cured — a step that matters more in Dallas summer than almost anywhere, because concrete that dries too fast never reaches its designed strength.

06

Clean-up and seal

Forms are pulled, the site is restored, and decorative finishes receive a penetrating or film-forming sealer once the slab has cured sufficiently.

Design ideas that work on Dallas lots

Most Dallas front yards are wider than they are deep, which makes a straight, narrow path feel like an afterthought. A few moves consistently improve the result:

  • Widen the walk to 48" at the front door and taper back to 36" — arrivals feel generous without adding much concrete.
  • Add a landing pad where the path meets the driveway so two people can pass without stepping into turf.
  • Curve gently around an existing tree instead of severing roots; mature canopy is worth more than a straight line.
  • Run a stamped or exposed-aggregate border against a plain broom field to frame the path and tie it to the brick.
  • Set low voltage lighting sleeves under the slab during the pour — retrofitting means cutting concrete later.
  • Keep colours mid-to-light. Dark integral colour looks striking in photos and hot on bare feet by noon in July.

Keeping it looking new

Concrete asks very little, but a little attention pays back over twenty years of Texas weather.

  • Rinse and mild-detergent wash once or twice a year; skip harsh acid cleaners on coloured surfaces.
  • Reseal decorative finishes every two to three years, plain broom finishes every four to five.
  • Keep sprinkler heads off the slab — constant wetting on one spot encourages efflorescence and staining.
  • Fill joints if they open up. An open joint lets water into the base, and a soft base is what causes panels to tilt.
  • Avoid de-icing salts on the rare ice day; sand gives traction without attacking the surface paste.
  • Trim roots at the edge before they lift a panel rather than after.

Questions we get asked

How thick should a concrete walkway be in Dallas?

Four inches over a compacted stone base is standard for foot traffic. Where a walkway crosses a driveway apron or might take a vehicle wheel, we step up to five or six inches with rebar reinforcement.

How long before I can walk on it?

Light foot traffic is usually fine after 24 to 48 hours depending on temperature and mix. Concrete reaches most of its design strength around 28 days, so heavy loads and furniture should wait.

Will it crack?

All concrete shrinks as it cures, so the goal is controlled cracking rather than no cracking. Properly spaced control joints, correct base preparation and reinforcement keep those cracks in the joints where they belong instead of across the middle of a panel.

Concrete walkway or pavers?

Pavers flex with soil movement and individual units can be lifted and reset, but they need edge restraint and joint sand maintenance and can settle unevenly. Poured concrete gives a continuous, weed-free, low-maintenance surface and — with stamping — can closely mimic the paver look at a lower long-term upkeep cost.

Can you match a new walkway to my existing driveway?

We match texture, joint pattern and colour as closely as materials allow. Exact colour matching to weathered concrete is never guaranteed, since existing slabs have years of UV and traffic on them; many homeowners choose a deliberate contrast band instead.

How wide should a front walkway be?

36 inches is the practical minimum. 42 to 48 inches lets two people walk side by side and reads far better against a typical Dallas front elevation.

Do you remove the old path?

Yes. Demolition, haul-off and disposal of the existing surface are quoted as a separate line so you can see the cost clearly.

What is the best finish for a shaded, damp side yard?

Exposed aggregate or a medium broom texture. Both hold grip when damp, and neither shows the algae shadowing that smooth troweled surfaces pick up in permanently shaded areas.
